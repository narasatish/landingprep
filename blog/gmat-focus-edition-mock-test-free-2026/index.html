GMAT Focus Edition: What Changed in 2024 (Still Current in 2026)

In 2023, GMAC (Graduate Management Admission Council) completely redesigned the GMAT. The new GMAT Focus Edition launched in November 2023 and replaced the older GMAT in 2024. Here's what's different:

Old GMAT (retired): Analytical Writing, Integrated Reasoning, Quantitative, Verbal. Score: 200–800. Duration: 3.5 hours.

New GMAT Focus Edition: Quantitative, Verbal Reasoning, Data Insights. Score: 205–805. Duration: 2 hours 15 minutes. Shorter, more practical, heavily focused on data analysis and problem-solving.

Most Indian MBA aspirants now take the GMAT Focus Edition. If you're applying in 2026, this is the test you'll take.

Scoring: 205–805 Scale Explained

The GMAT Focus uses a new scoring scale:

  • Minimum score: 205 (you'd almost have to try to score this low).
  • Maximum score: 805 (impossible to get all questions right due to question difficulty; ~97th percentile).
  • Competitive range: 650–750 (top-25% of test-takers). Most admitted MBA students at top schools score 650+.
  • Average score: ~550–600 (median test-taker).
  • Score breakdown: 205–805 is the composite score. Each section (Quant, Verbal, Data Insights) is scored separately (60–90 scale), then combined into the composite.
  • Quant score needed: 80+ is strong (85th percentile); 90+ is excellent (99th percentile).
  • Verbal score needed: 80+ is strong; 85+ is excellent.
  • Data Insights score needed: 80+ is strong (this is new; fewer people excel here yet).
  • Time to get score: Official scores available online 6–8 days after testing. Scores valid for 5 years.

Quantitative Section: 45 Minutes, 21 Questions

This is the hardest section for most Indian students (ironically, given our math background). Here's what to expect:

  • Question types: (1) Problem Solving (standard math questions with 5 multiple-choice options), (2) Data Sufficiency (logic questions: is the given data sufficient to answer?).
  • Topics: Arithmetic (fractions, percentages, rates), Algebra (equations, inequalities), Geometry (angles, areas, volume), Statistics (mean, median, standard deviation, probability).
  • Difficulty adaptation: Questions get harder or easier based on your performance. Miss 5 in a row? Questions get easier. Get 10 straight right? Questions get harder (adaptive testing).
  • Time management: ~2 minutes per question. Data Sufficiency questions are faster (~1.5 min) because you don't need to solve, just determine if data is sufficient.
  • Common traps: Wrong formulas, misreading percentages, forgetting about negative numbers. Practice these drills extensively.
  • Scoring: Both question types feed into the same section score (60–90).

Verbal Reasoning Section: 45 Minutes, 23 Questions

Verbal is where non-native English speakers often struggle. Here's the breakdown:

  • Question types: (1) Reading Comprehension (long passages, questions test understanding and inference), (2) Critical Reasoning (short argument passages; identify assumptions, weaknesses, strengthen argument), (3) Sentence Correction (grammar, idiom, clarity).
  • Question format: All multiple choice, 3–4 options per question.
  • Reading Comp: 2–4 passages, each 200–350 words. Questions ask: 'What's the main idea?', 'What does X imply?', 'Which statement weakens the argument?'.
  • Critical Reasoning: Similar to Reading Comp but shorter (50–100 words). Requires logical reasoning and spotting logical gaps.
  • Sentence Correction: Identify the grammatically correct or most clear version of a sentence. Tests grammar (subject-verb agreement, parallelism), idioms (common phrases), and clarity.
  • Time management: ~2 minutes per question. Budget 10 min for reading passages, rest for questions.
  • Common traps for Indians: Overcomplicating grammar rules, rushing through reading comp, missing subtle logical gaps.
  • Scoring: 60–90 scale; both question types feed into same section score.

Data Insights Section: 45 Minutes, 20 Questions (New!)

This is the newest section. It's where the old Integrated Reasoning evolved. Data Insights tests real-world analytical skills:

  • Question types: (1) Multi-Source Reasoning (interpret data from tables, graphs, text), (2) Table Analysis (data in a sortable table; answer questions), (3) Graphics Interpretation (bar charts, line graphs; fill-in-the-blank with options).
  • Key skill: Quickly extract data from messy sources and calculate/analyze it.
  • Example questions: 'Which region has the highest growth rate?' (requires calculating from data), 'True/False: Sales exceeded targets in Q2?' (requires reading graph).
  • Tools allowed: On-screen calculator (basic +, −, ×, ÷ only). You can't use it for complex calculations, but it speeds up arithmetic.
  • Time management: ~2.25 minutes per question. Multi-Source questions take longer (3+ min) because they require reading multiple sources.
  • Common traps: Misreading chart axes, forgetting units (e.g., thousands vs. millions), calculation errors despite the calculator.
  • Scoring: 60–90 scale; all three question types feed into same section score.

Free Official Mock Test: Where to Take It & What to Expect

GMAC offers a free official mock test to all test-takers. This is essential practice:

  1. Go to mba.com (official GMAC website).
  2. Create a free account (email + password).
  3. Click 'Free GMAT Focus Edition Practice Exams' or search 'GMAT Focus free mock'.
  4. Download the GMAT Focus Edition Full-Length Practice Exam (PDF or online version). There's also an interactive online version.
  5. Decide: take it under timed conditions (official testing experience) or untimed (practice mode).
  6. IMPORTANT: Set aside 2 hours 45 minutes (test + check-in time). No distractions.
  7. Take the full test in one sitting (don't break between sections except at scheduled breaks).
  8. After completing, review your answers. GMAC provides a detailed breakdown of your performance by section and topic.
  9. Note weak areas (e.g., 'Data Sufficiency' or 'Reading Comp'). Spend next 2 weeks drilling these.
  10. Take a second official mock test 2 weeks before your real test date.
  11. Track score improvement. A 100-point jump (e.g., 600 → 700) in 2 weeks is excellent and signals exam readiness.

12-Week GMAT Focus Study Plan for Indian Students

Here's a realistic study timeline. Adjust based on your starting level:

  • Weeks 1–2 (Foundation): Review basic math (percentages, ratios, probability). Review English grammar rules. Take the free mock test to establish a baseline score.
  • Weeks 3–4 (Topic Mastery - Quant): Master data sufficiency (trickiest part). Drill problem-solving with timed practice questions (OG problems). Target: 80+ accuracy in each area.
  • Weeks 5–6 (Topic Mastery - Verbal): Master Reading Comp by reading complex passages (The Economist, scientific journals). Practice Critical Reasoning. Do 20+ practice tests per day.
  • Weeks 7–8 (Data Insights Deep Dive): Practice multi-source reasoning and table analysis. Get comfortable with the on-screen calculator. This is often the weakest area; spend extra time.
  • Weeks 9–10 (Full-Length Mock Tests): Take a full-length mock every 3–4 days. Simulate test-day conditions (sit at a desk, no music, timer on). Score them. Analyze weak areas.
  • Weeks 11–12 (Final Drills + Test Day Prep): Drill weak question types daily (20–30 min). Review common traps. Take final practice mock 3 days before the real test. Get rest the day before; don't cram.
  • Study commitment: 10–15 hours per week for 12 weeks = 120–180 hours total. Fast-track (aggressive): 20 hours/week for 6–8 weeks also works.

Key Resources for GMAT Focus Prep (Free & Paid)

Here's what to use for your study plan:

  • Official Guide (OG): GMAC's 'Official Guide to the GMAT Focus Edition' (buy on Amazon or mba.com). ~500 real test questions. Use this more than any other resource. Cost: ~₹1,500–2,000.
  • Free Resources: Manhattan GMAT free blogs (strategy articles), GMAT Club (forums + flashcards), YouTube channels (Target Test Prep free content, GMAT Prep Now).
  • Paid prep courses (optional): Manhattan GMAT (₹30,000–50,000, high quality), Veritas Prep (₹25,000–45,000), Target Test Prep (₹15,000–25,000, best for Quant). All offer free trials; try before buying.
  • Flashcards: Anki (free app, upload GMAT decks) for vocabulary and formulas. 10 min/day of flashcards is passive but effective.
  • On-screen calculator practice: Use the one built into the mba.com practice exams. Don't overthink calculator use; basic arithmetic is still faster by hand.
  • One resource tip: Don't buy 5 courses. Pick ONE (e.g., Target Test Prep or Manhattan) and go deep. Breadth over depth is the mistake most students make.

Common GMAT Mistakes & How to Avoid Them

Learn from other test-takers' errors:

  • Rushing through Reading Comp: You'll miss subtle inferences. Spend 90 seconds reading, 1.5 min answering. Don't rush.
  • Overthinking Data Sufficiency: It's simpler than you think. Step 1: Is statement A sufficient? Step 2: Is statement B sufficient? Choose accordingly. Don't get lost in complex calculations.
  • Calculator dependency: On-screen calculator is slow and limited. Better to do math mentally or on scratch paper.
  • Ignoring the adaptive format: If you miss 3 in a row, difficulty drops. Don't let this demoralize you. It's the algorithm, not a sign of failure.
  • Not reading the question carefully: 'Which statement weakens?' vs. 'Which statement supports?'. One word changes the answer. Read twice.
  • Timing panic: If you have 30 seconds left and 3 questions remain, guess. Skipping questions (to save time) doesn't help; you're scored the same as a wrong answer. Better to attempt all.
  • Test day nerves: Practice under test conditions (full 2h 15m, no music, formal setting) at least 3 times. Familiarity = confidence.

After You Get Your Score: Next Steps

GMAT is just the first step to MBA admission. Here's what comes next:

  • Score acceptance: All MBA programs accept GMAT Focus scores. Scores are valid for 5 years. Your score is sent to schools you designate.
  • Retake decision: If you score below your target (e.g., target was 700, you got 650), retakes are available every 16 days. Most students retake once; retaking 3+ times looks bad to admissions.
  • School applications: Once you have a GMAT score, apply to MBA programs. Most schools want GMAT + essays + recommendations + work experience.
  • Application timeline: If you're targeting September 2026 MBA start (next intake), take GMAT by March 2026 and apply by April. First-round admission is June; second-round is August.
  • GMAT waiver: Some schools waive GMAT for high-performing candidates (e.g., if you have 10+ years work experience or strong academics). Ask schools if you're borderline.

Frequently asked questions

What is the GMAT Focus Edition, and how is it different from the old GMAT?
The **GMAT Focus Edition** launched in 2023 and is the current test. It's 2h 15m (down from 3.5h), has 3 sections (Quantitative, Verbal, Data Insights) instead of 4, and scores 205–805 (not 200–800). The old GMAT (with Analytical Writing) is retired. If you're taking the GMAT in 2026, you're taking the Focus Edition.
What is a good GMAT Focus score for MBA admission in 2026?
**650–750** is competitive for top-tier MBA programs. **700+** puts you in the top 25% (excellent). **750+** is exceptional (98th+ percentile). Average admitted student at a top-25 MBA program scores around 700–720. For tier-2 programs, 600–650 is acceptable.
How long should I study for the GMAT Focus Edition?
**Standard timeline**: 12 weeks, 10–15 hours per week = 120–180 hours. **Fast-track**: 6–8 weeks, 20+ hours per week (only if strong math/English background). **Slow-track**: 4–6 months if you need to brush up on fundamentals. Most Indian students spend 3–4 months.
Is the GMAT Focus harder or easier than the old GMAT?
Difficulty is comparable overall, but the focus has shifted: **easier on pure math**, **harder on data interpretation**, **data sufficiency is trickier** (requires more logic), **reading comprehension is still tough** (critical reasoning questions are more nuanced). Most students find it 'different,' not harder or easier.
Can I take a free official GMAT mock test?
Yes! GMAC offers **one free official full-length GMAT Focus practice exam** at mba.com. It's the most accurate predictor of your real test score. Take it early (week 2–3 of studying) to set a baseline; retake it 2 weeks before the real test.
What is the Data Insights section, and why is it new?
**Data Insights** replaced the old **Integrated Reasoning** section. It tests real-world data analysis: interpreting charts, tables, and graphs, then answering questions. It's heavily weighted in many MBA programs (equal to Quant and Verbal now). Most test-takers find it the toughest section because it's new and fewer practice resources exist.
Do all MBA programs accept GMAT Focus scores?
Yes. As of 2024, all top MBA programs globally accept GMAT Focus scores. Some programs still accept the old GMAT scores if you took it before 2024, but new test-takers must take GMAT Focus. The Focus score is not interchangeable with old GMAT scores; always use Focus for applications in 2026.
How many times can I retake the GMAT Focus?
You can retake every 16 days, unlimited times. However, **multiple retakes (3+) look bad to admissions officers**. Most successful applicants take it 1–2 times. Space retakes 4–6 weeks apart (time for improvement).
Should I take GMAT or GRE for MBA admission?
Both are accepted equally now. **Take GMAT if**: you're strong in math (GMAT Quant is harder but plays to your strength). **Take GRE if**: you love vocabulary and reading. Most Indian MBA aspirants choose GMAT because it's the traditional MBA test; familiarity helps confidence.
