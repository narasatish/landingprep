How IELTS Is Scored: Band Calculation Explained (2026)

IELTS scoring is complex: raw scores convert to bands using a mysterious algorithm, and the overall band is rounded in ways that trip up test-takers. Learn exactly how bands are calculated, what each score means, and why your 7.5 might not add up the way you think.

IELTS Scoring: The Big Picture

IELTS (International English Language Testing System) has four sections: Listening, Reading, Writing, and Speaking. Each section gets a band score from 1 to 9, and these are averaged to produce an Overall Band Score.

The critical thing to understand: raw scores don't directly convert to bands in a simple 1:1 ratio. IELTS uses a proprietary algorithm to convert your number of correct answers to a band score. This means you might get 38 out of 40 correct on Reading but receive an 8.0 band, while another test-taker with 37 correct might get a 7.5. The conversion is non-linear and varies slightly by test date.

Why? IELTS calibrates each test to ensure consistency. If a test is particularly difficult, the raw-to-band conversion is more generous. If it's easier, the conversion is stricter. This is called equating, and it ensures a band 7.0 from one test date is equivalent to a band 7.0 from another.

🔑
You cannot predict your exact band from your raw score. Conversions vary by test date. But you can estimate: generally, 30+/40 on Listening/Reading = 7.0+, and 35+/40 = 8.0+.

Writing Section: How It's Marked & Converted to a Band

Writing is not scored on raw points. Instead, two examiners independently score your two essays (Task 1: 150 words; Task 2: 250 words) on four criteria:

1. Task Achievement (TA) — Did you complete the task fully? Is your response relevant? (max 9 points) 2. Coherence & Cohesion (CC) — Is your writing organized? Do ideas flow logically? (max 9 points) 3. Lexical Resource (LR) — How sophisticated is your vocabulary? Do you use words accurately? (max 9 points) 4. Grammatical Range & Accuracy (GRA) — Is your grammar correct? Do you use a variety of structures? (max 9 points)

Weighting: Task 1 is worth 1x; Task 2 is worth 2x. So Task 2 matters twice as much.

Scoring process: 1. Examiner 1 scores both essays on all 4 criteria. 2. Examiner 2 independently scores both essays on all 4 criteria. 3. If scores differ by 1+ point, a senior examiner reviews. 4. Final score is the average of the two examiners' scores (or the senior's score if used). 5. The four criteria scores are averaged to produce your Writing Band Score (0.5 increments, so you could get 6.5, 7.0, 7.5, etc.)

  • Task Achievement: Fully address the prompt, organize ideas clearly, support with examples.
  • Coherence & Cohesion: Use linking words (however, furthermore, in addition), organize into paragraphs, make connections explicit.
  • Lexical Resource: Use synonyms, avoid repetition, use domain-specific vocabulary, spell correctly.
  • Grammatical Range & Accuracy: Vary sentence structures (complex + simple), use tenses correctly, avoid fragments.

Speaking Section: How Examiners Score Your Interview

Speaking is an 11–14 minute face-to-face interview (or video, post-COVID). One examiner scores you in real-time on four criteria, same as Writing:

1. Fluency & Coherence (FC) — Do you speak smoothly? Do ideas flow? Can you sustain speech without long pauses? (max 9) 2. Lexical Resource (LR) — How varied is your vocabulary? Do you paraphrase when you don't know a word? (max 9) 3. Grammatical Range & Accuracy (GRA) — Is your grammar correct? Do you use complex structures? (max 9) 4. Pronunciation — Is your accent clear? Can a native speaker understand you easily? (max 9)

Test structure: - Part 1 (4–5 min): Familiar topics (your job, hobbies, hometown). Warm-up. - Part 2 (3–4 min): You speak on a prepared card topic for 1–2 min; examiner asks follow-ups. - Part 3 (4–5 min): Deeper discussion of abstract topics related to Part 2.

Scoring: - Examiner scores you on the four criteria throughout all three parts. - No separate scores for each part—one overall score per criterion. - Final Speaking Band = average of the four criteria (0.5 increments).

  • Fluency: Speak at natural pace; pause to think, but don't stay silent >5 seconds. Use filler words naturally ('I mean,' 'you know'—sparingly).
  • Coherence: Connect ideas with linking words. Organize thoughts: 'First... second... finally.'
  • Lexical Resource: Use varied vocabulary; paraphrase if you forget a word. Avoid repetition.
  • Grammatical Range: Use complex sentences, past/present/future tenses accurately.
  • Pronunciation: Stress syllables correctly; pronounce individual sounds clearly (L vs. R, TH vs. D). Accent is fine if intelligible.

Overall Band Score: How Four Sections Average (With Rounding Rules)

This is where IELTS scoring gets tricky.

Your Overall Band Score is the average of all four sections (Listening, Reading, Writing, Speaking). BUT the rounding rules are non-intuitive.

Example 1: L 7.0 + R 8.0 + W 7.0 + S 7.5 = (7.0 + 8.0 + 7.0 + 7.5) ÷ 4 = 7.375 - Rounds to 7.5 (not 7.0, not 7.4)

Example 2: L 6.5 + R 7.0 + W 6.5 + S 6.5 = (6.5 + 7.0 + 6.5 + 6.5) ÷ 4 = 6.625 - Rounds to 6.5 (rounds down, not 7.0)

IELTS Rounding Rule: If the average ends in .25, it rounds to .5. If it ends in .5, it stays .5. If it ends in .75, it rounds to the next integer. Anything else is rounded to the nearest half-band.

More precisely: - .00–.24 = rounds down - .25–.74 = rounds to .5 - .75–.99 = rounds up to next integer

⚠️
This rounding rule is why a 6.5 + 6.5 + 6.5 + 7.0 (avg 6.625) gives you an Overall 6.5, not 7.0. It's frustrating, but it's the official rule.

What Each IELTS Band Score Means

IELTS provides official band descriptors. Here's what universities and employers understand by each score:

  • 9.0 (Expert User): Native-like English. Fluent, accurate, sophisticated. Rare. Almost no test-takers achieve this.
  • 8.5 (Very High Proficiency): Very fluent, occasional hesitations or slips. Suitable for top universities, professional roles requiring English.
  • 8.0 (Very Proficient): Fluent, understands complex ideas, rare errors. Accepted by most top 20 universities worldwide.
  • 7.5 (Proficient): Good fluency, mostly accurate, occasional misunderstandings. Accepted by top 50 universities.
  • 7.0 (Proficient): Operational proficiency, handles most situations, some hesitation. Accepted by most universities globally; minimum for many Master's programs.
  • 6.5 (Competent User): Adequate for most situations, some inaccuracy. Accepted by many universities, but often borderline for competitive programs.
  • 6.0 (Competent User): Adequate for most purposes, frequent hesitation and some errors. Accepted by some universities; often too low for competitive programs.
  • 5.5 (Modest User): Can manage simple communication; struggles with complex ideas. Below minimum for most universities.
  • 5.0 (Basic User): Basic communication only; struggles with most complex tasks. Below university threshold.
  • Below 5.0: Insufficient English for university study or professional roles.

Section Minimums: Can You Get an Overall 7 With Mixed Sections?

This is a critical question. Some universities don't just want a 7.0 overall; they want 7.0 in every section. Others are flexible.

Strict (section minimums required): - Most UK universities: Minimum 6.0–6.5 in every section, often 7.0+. - Medical/nursing programs: Often require 7.0+ in every section, especially Speaking and Writing. - Some Australian universities: No section <6.5 if Overall is 7.0+.

Flexible (only overall matters): - Most US universities: Only Overall Band matters. You could have L 7.5, R 8.0, W 6.5, S 6.5 = Overall 7.125 = 7.0 (acceptable). - Many Canadian universities: Overall Band matters most, but they prefer no section <6.0.

Strategy: Check your target university's specific requirements. If they list 'IELTS 7.0,' call admissions and ask: 'Is that 7.0 overall, or 7.0 in each section?' This can make a huge difference.

🔑
Read the fine print. 'IELTS 7.0' could mean Overall 7.0 (flexible) or 7.0 in all sections (strict). Ask your university.

Raw Scores vs. Band Scores: Why Conversions Matter

Here's a concrete example of how equating works:

Test Date A (easier): You get 37/40 on Reading. - Raw score: 37/40 - Conversion for this test: 37/40 = 8.0 band

Test Date B (harder): A different test-taker gets 36/40 on Reading. - Raw score: 36/40 - Conversion for this test: 36/40 = 8.0 band (because the test was harder)

Both get 8.0 bands, even though one got 37 correct and the other got 36. This is equating in action.

Why does this matter? You can't predict your band from a practice test. If you practice with older IELTS papers, you might score 38/40 on Reading and assume you'll get 8.0. But the actual test might be harder, so 38/40 gives you a 7.5 instead. Or easier, so 38/40 gives you 8.5.

Best practice: Use multiple practice tests to estimate your band range, not a single score. If you consistently get 35–39 on Reading across 5+ practice tests, you're probably in the 7.5–8.0 range on the real test.

  • Don't rely on one practice test: Do 5–10 practice tests and average your scores.
  • Track trends: If your practice scores are improving (35, 36, 37, 38), you're on the right track.
  • Expect variance: Even if you're strong, one test might give you 7.5 when you usually score 8.0. Variance exists.
  • Revisit old papers: Official IELTS practice papers (Books 1–17) are the most reliable because they use actual past papers with known conversions.

IELTS Academic vs. General Training: Scoring Differences

Most international students take IELTS Academic (for university admission). Some take General Training (for work visas, immigration). The scoring is identical, BUT the difficulty differs.

Academic: Reading & Writing are university-level (dense texts, academic topics). Harder.

General Training: Reading & Writing are work/survival-level (emails, advertisements, everyday texts). Easier.

Same conversion table? No. The raw-to-band conversion is different because the tests are calibrated separately. A 30/40 on Academic Reading might be a 6.5 band, but a 30/40 on General Training might be a 7.0 band (since General is easier).

Speaking & Listening: Identical content. Same conversion table for both.

  • Academic is harder: If you score 7.0 on Academic, you could likely score 7.5–8.0 on General Training with the same effort.
  • Universities want Academic: Most universities specify 'IELTS Academic' only. Check your requirement; don't accidentally take General Training.
  • General Training is for visas: If you're immigrating to Canada or Australia (not for study), General Training is often accepted and might give you a higher band.

How to Interpret Your IELTS Score Report

When you receive your score report, here's what to look for:

  • Test Report Form (TRF): Official document with your scores. Keep this safe; universities ask for it. Section Scores: Listening, Reading, Writing, Speaking (each 0–9, in 0.5 increments). Overall Band Score: Average of the four sections, rounded per IELTS rules. Validity: IELTS is valid for 2 years from the test date. After 2 years, it expires, and you must retake. Number of Candidates: Shows the percentile of test-takers you've surpassed. E.g., if you're in the 73rd percentile, 73% of test-takers scored lower than you. Score Threshold: IELTS publishes the raw score needed for each band on your report date. Use this to compare with practice tests.
  • Multiple attempts: Your TRF shows all scores from the past 2 years. If you've retaken, all appear. Universities usually take the highest Overall Band, but check the specific university's policy.

Retaking IELTS: When & How to Improve Your Score

Many students retake IELTS to improve from 6.5 to 7.0 or 7.0 to 7.5. Strategic retakes work if you target specific sections.

💡
Retaking is common. ~60% of IELTS test-takers retake at least once. It's not a sign of weakness; it's strategic improvement.
  1. Identify your weakest section: Is it Speaking (common for non-natives), Writing, Listening, or Reading?
  2. Focus prep on that section: If you're weak in Writing, do 20 practice essays over 2–3 months, not general prep.
  3. Realistic targets: Each band jump (6.5→7.0) typically requires 3–4 months of focused prep. Don't expect to jump 1.5 bands in 1 month.
  4. Retake timing: Book your retake 3–4 months after your first attempt, allowing time for targeted prep.
  5. Test date strategy: Apply to universities with your current score while prepping for a retake. If the retake improves your band, update your application later. If not, you've already applied with your best score.
  6. Which score counts?: Most universities take your highest Overall Band from any IELTS attempt in the past 2 years. Confirm with each university.

Frequently asked questions

Why can't I predict my IELTS band from my raw score?
IELTS uses **equating**: each test is calibrated separately based on difficulty. A harder test has a more generous conversion (more raw points = same band). IELTS wants a band 7.0 on one date to equal a 7.0 on another, even if raw scores differ slightly.
What does a 7.0 IELTS band mean for university admission?
7.0 is **acceptable** for most universities and many competitive programs. Top 10 universities often expect 7.5+. Check your specific university's requirements; many list minimums online.
If I score L 8.0, R 7.5, W 6.5, S 7.0, what's my overall?
(8.0 + 7.5 + 6.5 + 7.0) ÷ 4 = 7.25, which rounds to **7.5** overall. Not all universities accept mixed section scores; some require 7.0 in every section. Check your university's fine print.
Can I get into a top university with a 6.5 IELTS if my profile is strong?
It depends. If your profile (GPA, GRE, research) is exceptional and your university accepts 6.5, yes. But most top 20 universities want 7.0+. Don't assume a weak IELTS is acceptable; ask admissions directly.
Is IELTS Academic harder than General Training?
Yes, Academic Reading & Writing are university-level and harder. But the conversion tables differ; a 30/40 on Academic might be 6.5, while 30/40 on General might be 7.0. Universities specify 'IELTS Academic' for study; confirm you're taking the right version.
How long is an IELTS score valid?
**2 years** from your test date. After that, it expires. If your application is in year 3, you must retake. Plan your retake timeline accordingly.
Should I retake IELTS if I got 7.0 and my university requires 7.0?
No, unless your university specifically asks for higher. If 7.0 is the minimum and you have it, move forward. Retakes cost money and time; use that for other parts of your application instead.
What if I get 7.5 in Listening/Reading but 6.5 in Writing/Speaking?
Your overall would be (7.5 + 7.5 + 6.5 + 6.5) ÷ 4 = 7.0. Many universities are okay with this. But some (especially UK universities) require 6.5+ in all sections, or 7.0+ in Writing for academic work. Check your university's specific requirements.
